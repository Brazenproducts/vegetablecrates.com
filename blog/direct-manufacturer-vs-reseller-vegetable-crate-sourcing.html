Direct Manufacturer vs Reseller: Vegetable Crate Sourcing

Vegetablecrates Editorial • October 2026

When a grower-shipper or produce distributor decides to standardize on reusable plastic crates, the first strategic question is not which crate—it is who to buy from. The reusable packaging supply chain is crowded with resellers, brokers, and rebranders sitting between you and the factory. Understanding the difference between buying direct from a manufacturer and buying through an intermediary can swing your landed cost by double digits and determine whether you can get a crate built to your exact specification at all.

The Reseller Markup Reality

Most catalog distributors do not mold their own crates. They buy from overseas factories, warehouse inventory domestically, and resell with a margin that typically ranges from 20 to 40 percent over the factory price. For a stock crate that model offers convenience—you get fast domestic shipping and a single point of contact. But you pay for that convenience on every unit, and you inherit whatever specification the reseller chose to stock. If your commodity needs a specific vent pattern, wall height, or load rating, a reseller often cannot accommodate it without a prohibitive minimum.

What Direct Sourcing Changes

Buying direct from the factory removes the intermediary margin and, more importantly, opens the door to custom tooling. A direct manufacturer of reusable vegetable crates and produce packaging from Elipacko can adjust dimensions, ventilation, and reinforcement to match your pallet pattern and commodity, then hold that tooling for repeat orders. For high-volume buyers who will run the same crate for years, the per-unit savings and fit improvement compound quickly.

Understanding Import Duty and ADD

One reason some buyers hesitate on imported crates is anti-dumping duty (ADD). The United States has levied significant anti-dumping duties on certain plastic products originating from specific countries, which can add 100 percent or more to the landed cost and make the math unworkable. The country of manufacture matters enormously here. Elipacko manufactures in Thailand and Vietnam, which fall outside the scope of those anti-dumping orders—a 0 percent ADD position. That single factor can make direct import dramatically more competitive than it first appears, because you capture factory pricing without the punitive duty that affects product from other origins.

Landed Cost, Not Unit Price

Smart procurement compares landed cost, not sticker price. Landed cost includes:

  • Factory unit price after any volume tier.
  • Ocean freight and drayage, which favor nestable crate designs that ship more units per container.
  • Import duty—zero ADD exposure from Thailand or Vietnam origin is a material advantage.
  • Inventory carrying cost for the domestic buffer you choose to hold.

A nestable, properly specified crate from a 0 percent ADD origin frequently beats a domestically warehoused reseller crate on total landed cost even after accounting for longer lead time. Pair your crates with matching formats such as PP corrugated vegetable boxes and you can consolidate shipments to further improve container economics.

Managing Lead Time

The honest tradeoff with direct manufacturing is lead time. Custom tooling and ocean transit mean you plan in weeks, not days. The way to manage this is forecast-based ordering: commit to annual volume, schedule releases against your harvest and pack calendar, and hold a modest domestic safety stock. Elipacko's US-based manufacturing is in development, which over time will shorten lead times further and add a domestic replenishment option for buyers who need it.

Quality and Consistency at the Source

A concern buyers raise about direct import is quality consistency. The answer is to bake quality into the relationship rather than inspecting it in after the fact. Established manufacturers run their crates on dedicated tooling with controlled resin batches, which produces far more consistent wall thickness, color, and load performance than a reseller aggregating product from multiple unnamed factories. Ask for the manufacturer's process documentation, resin certifications, and a first-article sample from your actual tooling. Because a direct relationship gives you traceability back to a single plant, resolving any issue is faster and cleaner than chasing a broker who cannot identify which factory produced a given lot. Over a multi-season program, that traceability is worth as much as the price savings.

Due Diligence Checklist

Before you place a direct order, confirm the resin is FDA-compliant for food contact, request documentation of the manufacturing origin for duty purposes, ask for sample crates and drawings, and verify the supplier can hold your tooling for reorders. A manufacturer that can answer all four questions clearly is one you can build a multi-year program around.
