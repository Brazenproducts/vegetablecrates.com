Vegetable Crate Pooling vs Ownership: A Cost Model

Vegetablecrates Editorial • October 2026

Two Paths to Reusable Crates

Produce operations moving away from disposable corrugated or wax boxes face a strategic fork: rent crates through a pooling network, or buy and own a fleet of reusable crates outright. Both approaches eliminate single-use waste, but they allocate cost, risk, and control very differently. For procurement teams, the right answer depends on shipment volume, lane structure, and how much operational control the business wants to retain.

This article builds a simple per-trip cost model so logistics and finance teams can compare the two paths on a common basis using durable vegetable crates and produce packaging from Elipacko as the owned-asset benchmark.

The Pooling Cost Structure

In a pooling model, a third party owns the crates and charges a per-trip or per-day rental fee plus transfer and cleaning charges. The appeal is obvious: no capital outlay, no asset management, and the pool operator handles retrieval and sanitation. The trade-offs are recurring fees that never end, limited crate customization, and exposure to availability shortfalls during peak harvest when every grower needs crates at once.

  • Per-trip rental fee: typically recurring for the life of the program
  • Issue and transfer charges: added at each pool touch point
  • Cleaning and reconditioning: bundled into pool fees
  • Shrinkage liability: often passed back to the shipper

The Ownership Cost Structure

Owning crates inverts the economics. A distributor pays once for the fleet and then amortizes that cost across every trip the crate makes. A well-built PP crate lasts five to ten years and survives hundreds of cycles, so the capital cost spreads thin. Against a rental fee, owned crates often reach cost parity within 18 to 30 months, after which every subsequent trip is nearly free of packaging cost.

The model math is straightforward. Divide the landed crate cost by the realistic number of trips over its service life. A crate costing a modest amount up front that completes 300 trips carries a per-trip packaging cost measured in cents. Direct-from-manufacturer sourcing sharpens this further: because Elipacko manufactures in Thailand and Vietnam at a 0 percent antidumping duty rate and sells directly to US buyers, the acquisition cost that feeds the per-trip calculation starts lower than distributor-markup pricing.

Where Ownership Wins

Ownership favors operations with predictable, high-frequency lanes and the ability to recover empties. When a distributor controls its crates, it also controls specification. Custom internal dimensions, color coding for SKU segregation, and hot-stamped branding all become possible. For buyers pairing crates with other reusable assets, sourcing from one manufacturer that also builds PP corrugated vegetable boxes simplifies the vendor relationship and standardizes resin and compliance across the fleet.

Where Pooling Wins

Pooling remains attractive for operations with highly variable volume, one-way lanes where empties cannot be recovered, or seasonal spikes that would leave an owned fleet idle most of the year. The key is to compare the all-in recurring pool cost against the amortized owned-crate cost over the same horizon, including a realistic shrinkage allowance for the owned fleet.

Building Your Own Model

Finance teams should gather four inputs: annual trip volume, landed crate cost, expected service life in trips, and annual shrinkage rate. With these, a five-year comparison between pooling fees and amortized ownership becomes a clear side-by-side. For most steady, high-volume produce operations with recoverable lanes, ownership of a durable, FDA-compliant PP crate fleet delivers the lower total cost of ownership and the added benefit of complete control over specification and supply.
